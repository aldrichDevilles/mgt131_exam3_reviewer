Module 4.1: Foundation of Organizational Structure

What is Organizational Structure? It defines how jobs are formally divided, grouped, and coordinated inside a company.

1. The Six Key Elements of Structure

  1. Work Specialization (Division of Labor): Breaking a big job into tiny, repetitive steps done by different people (like an assembly line).
    Watch the curve! At first, specialization boosts productivity (economies of specialization). Push it too far, and people get bored, stressed, and sloppy—causing productivity to drop (human diseconomies).
  2. Departmentalization: How jobs are grouped together—by function (HR, Finance), product (iPhone, Mac), territory/geography (Luzon, Visayas), process (cutting, sewing), or customer (retail, corporate).
  3. Chain of Command: The unbroken line of authority from the CEO down to the newest intern ("To whom am I responsible?").
    • Authority: The formal right of a manager to give orders and expect them to be obeyed.
    • Unity of Command: You should only have one direct boss so you don't get conflicting orders.
  4. Span of Control: How many employees a single manager can efficiently and effectively direct.
    Wide span (e.g., 8 per boss) = Fewer management layers, cheaper, faster decisions, flatter org. Narrow span (e.g., 4 per boss) = Tall org, expensive, slow communication, micromanagement.
  5. Centralization vs. Decentralization: Where decision-making lies. Centralized = only top bosses decide. Decentralized = lower-level staff can make decisions too (more autonomy).
  6. Formalization: How standardized jobs are. High formalization = thick rulebooks, zero wiggle room. Low formalization = freedom to figure out how to do your work.

2. Common Organizational Designs

  • Simple Structure: Low departmentalization, wide span of control, centralized in one person (usually the owner), little formalization. Fast, flexible, cheap, clear accountability—great for small shops, breaks down as you grow.
  • Bureaucracy: Highly routine tasks, high specialization, very formalized rules, functional departments, centralized authority, narrow spans of control, strict chain of command. Super efficient for standard tasks, terrible at adapting to weird exceptions.
  • Matrix Structure: Combines functional and product departmentalization. Breaks Unity of Command because employees have two bosses (a functional manager AND a product manager). Great for complex coordination, causes power struggles and role confusion.

3. New Design Options

  • Team Structure: Breaks down department walls and decentralizes decision-making directly to self-managed work teams (e.g., Whole Foods Market, where every store is made of autonomous teams).
  • Virtual Organization: A tiny core headquarters that outsources almost all major functions (manufacturing, R&D, marketing, sales) to outside specialists. Highly centralized, little to no departmentalization.
  • Boundaryless Organization: Eliminates both internal walls and external barriers (geography, suppliers, customers) using globalization, strategic alliances, customer links, and telecommuting.

4. Two Extreme Models: Mechanistic vs. Organic

Mechanistic Model (The Machine)

  • High specialization
  • Rigid departmentalization
  • Clear chain of command
  • Narrow spans of control
  • Centralization
  • High formalization
  • Limited info network (mostly downward)

Organic Model (The Living Organism)

  • Cross-functional & cross-hierarchical teams
  • Free flow of information
  • Wide spans of control
  • Decentralization
  • Low formalization
  • Flat structure
  • High participation in decision-making

5. Four Forces That Influence Design

  1. Strategy (Structure follows Strategy):
    • Innovation Strategy (e.g., Apple) → Organic (loose, low formalization, decentralized).
    • Cost-Minimization Strategy (e.g., Walmart) → Mechanistic (tight control, high specialization & formalization).
    • Imitation Strategy (e.g., fast fashion copying designers) → Both Mechanistic & Organic (tight control on current products, loose control for new undertakings).
  2. Organization Size: Larger organizations become more mechanistic (more specialization, departments, vertical levels, rules), though size matters less once a company is already huge.
  3. Technology (Degree of Routineness): Routine tasks = Mechanistic (manuals, rules). Nonroutine/customized tasks = Organic.
  4. Environment (3 Dimensions):
    • Capacity: Can it support growth? (Abundant vs. Scarce)
    • Volatility: How unstable is it? (Stable vs. Dynamic)
    • Complexity: How diverse/concentrated are outside elements? (Simple vs. Complex)
    • Rule: The more scarce, dynamic, and complex the environment, the more Organic the structure should be.

6. Impact on Employee Behavior

  • Work Specialization: Higher productivity, but lower job satisfaction.
  • Span of Control: No proven relationship with performance (some people like autonomy; others want a boss nearby).
  • Decentralization: More autonomy & participative decision-making = higher job satisfaction.
  • Bottom Line: Individual differences (experience, personality, task, cultural norms) moderate how structure affects people.

Module 4.2: Work Design and Technology

Work Design aims to create jobs and work groups that generate high levels of both employee fulfillment and productivity. Technology is simply how an organization transfers inputs into outputs.

1. Three Approaches to Work Design

  1. Engineering Approach: Focuses on efficiency and task simplification → traditional, repetitive jobs.
  2. Motivational Approach: Anchored on motivational theories → tries to enrich the work experience.
  3. Sociotechnical Systems Approach: Optimizes both social and technical aspects → gave birth to self-managed teams (multi-skilled members doing interrelated tasks).

2. Issues Related to Technology & Work

  • Continuous Improvement Processes: Non-stop pressure to tweak and improve processes can cause employee anxiety and stress.
  • Process Reengineering: Radically rethinking and redesigning how the company creates value, throwing out antiquated operations.
    • Distinctive Competencies: What the company does better than anyone else (location, quality, tech support).
    • Core Processes: Processes that transform materials, capital, info, and labor into things customers value.
    • Horizontal Processes: Using cross-functional & self-managed teams and cutting out layers of middle management.
    • Implication to Employees: Survivors don't keep the same old jobs—they get wider skill requirements, more customer/supplier contact, bigger challenges, more responsibility, and higher pay.

3. OB in an E-World (E-Organizations)

Know the Difference:
• E-commerce: Just the sales side of electronic business.
• E-business: The full breadth of activities in an Internet-based enterprise.
• E-organization (e-org): Applying e-business concepts to all organizations (even non-profits/schools).

Three Underlying Components:

  • Internet: Worldwide network of interconnected computers.
  • Intranet: An organization's private internal internet (employees only).
  • Extranet: Extended intranet accessible to selected employees and authorized outsiders (like suppliers or clients).

Implications for Individual Behavior:

  • Motivation & Cyberloafing: Using company internet during work hours to browse non-work sites or send personal emails.
  • Ethics: Electronic surveillance pits the company's desire for control against the employee's right to privacy.

Implications for Group Behavior:

  • Decision Making: Team-based communities make group models more relevant; action models replace slow rational models.
  • Communication: Breaks down status hierarchies (anyone can message anyone), but causes communication overload and constant interruptions.
  • Politics & Networking: Chat rooms/message boards help employees build career networks.
  • Interpersonal Relationships: Extroverts use the internet to widen their social network; for Introverts, it actually undermines their offline social interaction.

4. Work Redesign Options

  • Job Rotation (Cross Training): Moving workers periodically from one task to another.
    Pros: Reduces boredom, increases motivation, gives management scheduling flexibility.
    Cons: Higher training costs, drops productivity right when the worker was getting efficient, forces teams to adjust, takes up supervisor time.
  • Job Enlargement (Horizontal Expansion): Adding more tasks at the same level. Fixes lack of variety, but does little to add challenge or meaningfulness.
  • Job Enrichment (Vertical Expansion): Giving the worker a complete activity, more freedom/independence, more responsibility, and direct feedback so they can self-correct.

5. Work Schedule Options

  • Flextime: Discretion over arrival/departure times. Reduces absenteeism and boosts productivity. Drawback: Not applicable to every job (e.g., receptionists, assembly lines).
  • Job Sharing: Two+ people split one 40-hour job (e.g., 8am–12nn and 1pm–5pm). Attracts skilled workers like retirees or parents with young kids. Drawback: Hard to find compatible pairs who can coordinate seamlessly.
  • Telecommuting: Working from home at least 2 days a week via computer linked to the office. Great flexibility. Drawback: For employees with high social needs, it increases feelings of isolation and lowers job satisfaction.

Module 4.3: Organizational Culture & Change

Organizational Culture is a system of shared meaning held by members that distinguishes one organization from others.

1. Seven Primary Characteristics of Culture

  1. Innovation and risk-taking: Encouraged to be innovative and take risks.
  2. Attention to detail: Expected to show precision and analysis.
  3. Outcome orientation: Focus on results rather than the techniques used to get them.
  4. People orientation: Decisions consider the effect on people inside the org.
  5. Team orientation: Work organized around teams rather than individuals.
  6. Aggressiveness: Competitive and aggressive rather than easygoing/cooperative.
  7. Stability: Emphasizes maintaining the status quo rather than growth.

2. Strong Culture & Culture as a Liability

A Strong Culture exists when core values are both intensely held and widely shared. Key result: lower employee turnover.

When Culture Becomes a Liability (3 Ways):

  • Barrier to Change: Shared values clash with what the org needs to stay effective in a changing world.
  • Barrier to Diversity: Strong pressure to conform limits acceptable values and styles.
  • Barrier to Acquisitions & Mergers: Even if financials look great, mergers fail if the two cultures don't match.

3. Creating, Sustaining & Learning Culture

  • How Culture Begins (Founders): Founders (1) hire/keep only people who think like them, (2) indoctrinate/socialize them, and (3) act as role models.
  • Keeping Culture Alive (3 Forces): (1) Selection Practices (hiring for fit), (2) Actions of Top Management, and (3) Socialization (onboarding new hires).
  • How Employees Learn Culture (4 Ways):
    • Stories: Narratives about founders, rule-breaking, rags-to-riches, coping with past mistakes.
    • Rituals: Repetitive sequences of activities reinforcing key values (e.g., company chants, annual awards).
    • Material Symbols: Office layout, executive cars, dress code, perks—shows who is important and what behavior is expected.
    • Language: Unique acronyms and jargon that unite members once learned.
  • 5 Steps to an Ethical Culture: (1) Be a visible role model, (2) Communicate ethical expectations, (3) Provide ethical training, (4) Visibly reward ethical acts & punish unethical ones, (5) Provide protective mechanisms (e.g., safe whistleblower channels).

4. Organizational Change: Forces & Resistance

6 Forces for Change: Nature of the workforce (diversity, aging), Technology, Economic shocks, Competition, Social trends, and World politics.

Goals of Planned Change: (1) Improve the org's ability to adapt to its environment, and (2) Change employee behavior.

Individual Resistance (5)

  • Habit
  • Security
  • Economic factors (fear of pay cut)
  • Fear of the unknown
  • Selective information processing

Organizational Resistance (6)

  • Structural inertia (built-in stability)
  • Limited focus of change (subsystems)
  • Group inertia (group norms)
  • Threat to expertise
  • Threat to established power relationships
  • Threat to established resource allocation

8 Ways to Overcome Resistance: Education & communication, Participation, Building support & commitment, Developing positive relationships, Implementing changes fairly, Manipulation & cooptation, Selecting people who accept change, and Coercion (threats/force).

5. Approaches to Managing Organizational Change

  • Lewin's Three-Step Model:
    1. Unfreezing: Overcoming the status quo (increase driving forces or decrease restraining forces).
    2. Movement: Transitioning to the desired new state.
    3. Refreezing: Stabilizing the new change so it sticks.
  • Action Research: Systematic data collection to select a change action. 5 Steps: Diagnosis → Analysis → Feedback → Action → Evaluation.
  • Organizational Development (OD) Techniques (6):
    • Sensitivity Training: Changing behavior through unstructured group interaction.
    • Survey Feedback: Assessing attitudes via surveys to identify and solve perception discrepancies.
    • Process Consultation: Bringing in an outside consultant to help understand process events.
    • Team Building: High-interaction group activities to build trust and openness.
    • Intergroup Development: Changing attitudes, stereotypes, and perceptions that different groups have of each other.
    • Appreciative Inquiry: Accentuating the positive (what the org does right) instead of hunting for problems.
  • Creating a Culture for Change: Stimulating a culture of innovation & creating a Learning Organization.
